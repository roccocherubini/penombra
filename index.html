Penombra

penombra, Italian: half-light

Descent images of the lunar south pole, in the light a lander will really meet.

Physically based, rendered from NASA elevation data and the real position of the Sun, and delivered with the exact answer for every pixel. Navigation software can be tested here before it flies.

The problem

At the south pole, the Sun never climbs

Near the lunar south pole the Sun circles the horizon and stays within a few degrees of it. Light arrives from the side, so even small relief throws long shadows, and those shadows swing round as the Sun moves. Craters, rocks and slopes look very different from the same features under a high Sun.

A lander that navigates by its camera has to recognise craters, track the ground and spot hazards in exactly this light. Testing that software needs images taken in it, with the right answer known for every pixel. Real descent images of the polar regions are few, and none come with that answer.

What Penombra adds

What the best map cannot show

Elevation map only Penombra

Same place, same Sun, same camera. Drag the handle. At 500 m one pixel covers about 40 cm of ground, twelve times finer than the 5 m grid of the elevation map.

The most detailed public elevation map of the site, from NASA's Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter, has one height every 5 m. Close to the ground that is far too coarse: rendered on its own, the surface looks smooth and blurred, with the triangles of the map showing through.

Penombra adds what the map cannot resolve, with sizes and numbers taken from measurements of the Moon.

Checked against reality

Real photo vs Penombra

NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter photographed the descent target with the Sun just above the horizon. Penombra rendered the same square kilometre for the same instant.

Point at a crater: the ring marks the same spot in both
86–99%of pixels in the same light or shadow
< 10%difference in brightness where the Sun hits
1.3×small shadows vs the photo (2.3× before tuning on these photos; 1.3× on 11 photos not used to tune)

The large shadows come from NASA's elevation data and the Sun's position: the elevation map alone scores the same, 88–99%. Penombra adds the fine grain. Craters under about 20 m and boulders are generated, not copied: right number and size, different places. Four photos, Sun 1.2° to 1.4° high, 1 pixel = 1 m.

The same question, asked of real photos

When the Sun turns, real photos stop matching. Penombra predicts when.

Fifteen orbiter photos of the site, each with the Sun in a different direction, were matched pair by pair with the same software. Then Penombra rendered the same pairs.

Demo v0 · interactive

Same place, Sun turned 60°: the lander is lost.

A lander finds its position by matching its camera to a map of the site. In Penombra's test that always works while the Sun has moved less than 20°, about a day and a half at the south pole, and never from 60°. Real photos agree: pairs of orbiter images still match with the Sun 28° apart, and no longer from 39°.

How it is built

Public data and physics, checked on every frame

Terrain

Real elevation maps, near and far

NASA LOLA data at 5 m around the site, 80 m out to 30 km and 400 m out to 150 km, on a curved Moon. One seamless surface, so distant ridges cast their shadows too.

Sun

The real Sun on a real date

Sun position from NASA's SPICE ephemerides. Each descent is planned for a moment when the Sun is truly above the local skyline, not just above the flat horizon.

Light

Lunar reflectance

The Lunar-Lambert reflectance law with McEwen's coefficients, rendered with Blender Cycles, including light bounced between slopes. Pixel values are calibrated reflectance.

Detail

What the map cannot resolve

Small craters, boulders and roughness generated from published lunar statistics. A fixed seed keeps the same world in every frame, so every frame agrees with the others.

Camera

A navigation camera, with its flaws

A generic 12-bit camera: optics, automatic exposure, lens blur, vignetting, noise, and motion blur computed from the real movement during each exposure.

Trajectory

A plausible descent

A polynomial descent path of the kind real landers follow, written in Python and in MATLAB with identical results, with checks on thrust and clearance above the ground. The camera is aimed at the target; the vehicle's attitude is not modelled.

Who it is for

For anyone who has to land by sight

Lander navigation teams

Test crater matching, visual odometry and hazard detection in the light they will meet, and measure the errors against exact answers.

Researchers and students

A benchmark with known ground truth for papers and theses, and training and test data for learned detectors.

Camera and sensor teams

Check exposure, dynamic range and noise on scenes that are half in deep shadow.

A sequence for your mission

Your landing site, your trajectory, your date and your camera, with the same ground truth. A 24-frame sequence renders in about 11 minutes on a laptop, so changes are quick.

Where it stands

Version 0, autumn 2026

One site, one date, one trajectory so far. Done since the first images: a navigation demonstration with 196 position fixes measured against the ground truth, a comparison with four real photos from NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter, and 104 pairs of real photos matched against each other and against Penombra. Next come more sites and lighting conditions, oblique descents, and a full camera model for the orbital photos.

  • Rocks and soil share the same reflectance; there is no Earthshine.
  • Brightness is checked against real photos only for a camera looking straight down; inside deep shadow the picture is mostly rendering noise.
  • The distant terrain is smoother than the terrain near the target.
  • The camera is generic, not a model of a specific flight camera.
  • Small craters and boulders are realistic in number and size, not the real ones.
