How does a lander know where it is?

It has a camera. And a map.

ALT2,000 mSUN1.4° above the horizon

Step 1

It carries a map of the landing site.

An image of the area seen from above, prepared before launch. For every point on it, the lander knows the exact position on the Moon.

Step 2

It takes a photo.

Here it is 1,000 m above the landing point. The camera sees about 830 m of ground, the dashed square on the map.

Step 3

It finds the same craters and rocks in both.

Software picks out thousands of small details and pairs them up. Here 1,887 pairs are right. Each line joins a point in the photo to the same point on the map.

Step 4

It works out where it is.

From those pairs it computes its own position: 0.3 m from the true one. Penombra knows the true position exactly, so it can grade every answer. This is the best case: the map shows every small crater the camera sees.

  
The catch

The map was made on another day.

At the south pole the Sun never climbs. It circles the horizon, about 12° a day, and every shadow turns with it. Scroll to move the Sun on the map.

What it means

The low Sun is fine. The wrong day is not.

0.5 mPosition error with a map lit like landing day, even with the Sun 1.4° above the horizon. A best case: this map shows every small crater the camera sees.
±1.5 daysRoughly how long a map stays usable at the pole. In this test it always works up to 20° of Sun movement and never from 60°. Real orbiter photos agree: pairs still match 28° apart, and no longer from 39°.
4 of 28The edge. With the Sun moved by 30° the lander found its position 4 times in 28. It never gave a wrong answer: when it could not tell, it said so.

The fix is a map lit for landing day, rendered from the 3D terrain for the right date and Sun position.

There is a limit, and Penombra measured it too. Before flight the terrain is known from the 5 m elevation map, without the small craters and rocks the camera sees from close up. With a map rendered from that alone, in the right light, the lander still finds itself, but 1 to 6 m off instead of 0.5 m (9 m at worst), and with fewer and fewer matches the lower it gets. Closing that gap is an open question.

How the test was done
Site
Rim of Shackleton crater, the same terrain as the sample dataset: NASA LOLA elevation data with Penombra's small craters, boulders and roughness.
Descent
Vertical, camera pointing straight down, 7 altitudes from 2,000 m to 500 m above the landing point. Simulated 12-bit camera with noise.
Map
View from directly above, 2.6 km across at 1 m per pixel. Rendered with the Sun at the same height, turned by 0° to 180° around the horizon. A second map, rendered from the elevation data alone, was tried in the landing-day light.
Navigation
SIFT features in photo and map, matched and turned into a position with PnP and RANSAC. Starting position known to ±150 m, altimeter accurate to 1%.
Grading
A position counts as found when it is less than 5 m from the truth and rests on at least 12 consistent pairs. Every pair is checked against the ground truth.
Limits
Photo and map come from the same simulated terrain, small craters included, so 0.5 m is a best case. One site, one classic algorithm, vertical descent only. The added detail is tuned against real orbiter photos, but it is statistical: the right number of small craters, not the real ones. Learned methods may tolerate more; Penombra is built to measure exactly that.
