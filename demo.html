How does a lander know where it is?

Coming down to the Moon, it has to find its own way.

It has a camera. And a map.

Step 1

It carries a map of the landing site.

An image of the area seen from above, made before launch from orbital photos. For every point on it, the lander knows the exact position on the Moon.

Step 2

It takes a photo.

Here it is 1,000 m above the landing point. The camera sees about 830 m of ground, the dashed square on the map.

Step 3

It finds the same craters and rocks in both.

Software picks out thousands of small details and pairs them up. Here 1,800 pairs are right. Each line joins a point in the photo to the same point on the map.

Step 4

It works out where it is.

From those pairs it computes its own position: 0.3 m from the true one. Penombra knows the true position exactly, so it can grade every answer.

  
The catch

The map was made on another day.

At the south pole the Sun never climbs. It circles the horizon, about 12° a day, and every shadow turns with it. Scroll to move the Sun on the map.

What it means

The low Sun is fine. The wrong day is not.

0.5 mPosition error with a map lit like landing day, even with the Sun 1.4° above the horizon.
±1.5 daysHow long a map stays usable at the pole: beyond about 20° of Sun movement, the lander gets lost.
6 mThe dangerous case. At the edge, one answer was wrong while the system looked confident.

The fix is a map lit for landing day. Penombra renders it from the 3D terrain, for any date and Sun position, and tests the navigation against it before flight, with the exact answer for every pixel.

How the test was done
Site
Rim of Shackleton crater, the same terrain as the sample dataset: NASA LOLA elevation data with Penombra's small craters, boulders and roughness.
Descent
Vertical, camera pointing straight down, 7 altitudes from 2,000 m to 500 m above the landing point. Simulated 12-bit camera with noise.
Map
View from directly above, 2.6 km across at 1 m per pixel. Rendered with the Sun at the same height, turned by 0° to 180° around the horizon.
Navigation
SIFT features in photo and map, matched and turned into a position with PnP and RANSAC. Starting position known to ±150 m, altimeter accurate to 1%.
Grading
A position counts as found when it is less than 5 m from the truth and rests on at least 12 consistent pairs. Every pair is checked against the ground truth.
Limits
One site, one classic algorithm, maps rendered with the same model rather than real orbital photos, vertical descent only. Learned methods may tolerate more; Penombra is built to measure exactly that.
